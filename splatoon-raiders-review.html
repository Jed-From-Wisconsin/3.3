Splatoon Raiders: Review

Iconic Multiplayer Shooter Receives Single-Player Spin-Off

The Charges: Repetitive combat, bland level design, and a grind that forgets to be fun.

The Case Before the Court

Splatoon is a young franchise by Nintendo standards. It debuted in 2015, and it has already earned a place in the gaming canon with a distinct identity, a devoted fanbase, and spin-offs. That rise is a credit to the series, but it also raises the stakes. When a franchise this fresh starts branching out, the court expects each new case to justify its existence. Splatoon Raiders takes the stand to do exactly that.

The Plot

The story is short and serves mostly as a premise for the action. Players take the role of a new recruit sent into dangerous territory to take on a growing threat, with a small supporting cast along for the ride. It has the charm and personality the series is known for, but the narrative is a means to an end rather than a reason to keep playing. The court finds it inoffensive, if forgettable.

The Gameplay Loop

The structure is simple. Drop into a stage, fight your way through enemies and objectives, collect rewards, and return to prepare for the next run. Upgrades and new gear feed back into the cycle, and the game expects you to repeat it many times over. The loop is clear and easy to understand, which makes it all the more important that it be fun to repeat.

Evidence for the Defense

To its credit, the moment-to-moment feel is solid. Movement carries the series’ signature fluidity, and swimming through ink remains one of the most satisfying traversal mechanics in the genre. Combat is responsive, weapons feel good in the hand, and the core mechanics are as polished as you’d expect from this franchise. If this were a trial about game feel alone, Raiders would walk free.

Evidence for the Prosecution

The level design is bland. Stages lack the creativity and personality that make Splatoon’s multiplayer maps memorable. They function, but they rarely surprise, and too many areas blur together into a sea of corridors and arenas that exist only to hold enemies.

The grind is not fun. The game asks for a lot of repetition, and it rewards that repetition with incremental progress that rarely feels meaningful. Each run feels less like discovery and more like clocking in for a shift.

The combat gets repetitive. The fundamentals are strong, but the encounters built on them don’t evolve enough to keep things fresh. Compare this to Salmon Run, where unpredictable enemy waves, shifting stages, and the chaos of cooperation keep every round feeling distinct. Compare it to Side Order, which wrapped its repeated runs in randomized upgrades and build variety that made each attempt feel like a new experiment. Raiders has the repetition of those modes without the variety that justified them. The result is a game that borrows the structure of its siblings but leaves the substance behind.

The Verdict

Splatoon Raiders clearly wants to be infinitely grindable. Everything about its design points toward a game you return to again and again, chasing the next upgrade for hours on end. But a game can only be infinitely grindable if the grind itself is satisfying. The best examples of the genre make you want to run it back because the act of playing is its own reward. Raiders fails that test. The foundation is strong, but the structure built on top of it asks for endless commitment without giving players a reason to offer it.

Final Judgment: Guilty of wasted potential. Sentenced to time served, with the possibility of parole should future updates address the evidence.

Court is adjourned.
